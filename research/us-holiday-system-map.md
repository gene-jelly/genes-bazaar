# The US Holiday, PTO, and School-Calendar System: A Reference Map

A field guide for anyone who can never keep track of which days off come from which level of government, and who actually gets them. Companion reference for the School Calendar Fragmentation dataset.

Compiled April 2026. All figures cited inline. Where numbers look stale or contested, this document says so.

---

## 1. The four layers of US holidays

The United States does not have a single "holiday system." It has four layers stacked on top of each other, and none of them automatically cascade into the others. This is the single most important thing to understand: **a federal holiday is not a national holiday**. It is a holiday for the federal workforce and for institutions that have chosen to track the federal calendar (banks, bond markets, most public schools, most large private employers). Nothing in federal law closes a private business or a school on any of these days.

**Layer 1 — Federal holidays.** There are 11 federal holidays, codified at [5 U.S.C. § 6103](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/6103). The statute is narrow: it defines which days are "legal public holidays" for federal employees and the District of Columbia. Automatic beneficiaries: federal civilian workers (paid day off), federal courts, post offices, the Federal Reserve, and by extension banks and US securities markets that follow the Fed calendar. Nothing else is automatic. Private employers choose whether to observe. States choose whether to observe. Schools choose whether to close. "National holiday" has no legal meaning in the US — it's a colloquialism.

**Layer 2 — State holidays.** Every state designates its own legal holidays by statute. States almost always mirror the federal 11 (with small exceptions — e.g., several Southern states historically did not recognize MLK Day as a standalone holiday) and then add their own. Notable additions: **Patriots' Day** (third Monday in April) in Massachusetts and Maine, marking Lexington & Concord; [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriots%27_Day). **Cesar Chavez Day** (March 31) in California, Colorado, Texas — though in March 2026 the UFW halted celebrations following abuse allegations and several jurisdictions renamed it Farmworkers Day; [CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/politics/2026/03/cesar-chavez-day-renamed/). **Emancipation Day** (April 16) in DC, marking the 1862 Compensated Emancipation Act — made a legal DC holiday in 2005 by DC Act 15-082; [DC Code § 1-612.02](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/1-612.02). State employees get these automatically. Private employers are not legally required to close for state holidays in any state; observance is voluntary. Schools usually follow the state calendar because school districts are creatures of state law.

**Layer 3 — Local/municipal holidays.** Cities, counties, and especially **school districts** can add observances beyond state law. This is where religious-pluralism days live. New York State in 2023 made Lunar New Year a public-school holiday statewide, and NYC schools added Diwali (first observed November 1, 2024) — [Chalkbeat](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2023/6/26/23774160/nyc-2023-2024-school-calendar-update-days-off-easter-passover-eid-diwali/), [QNS](https://qns.com/2024/11/nyc-public-schools-close-for-first-ever-diwali-holiday-marking-historic-milestone-for-south-asian-community/). NYC also observes Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha. Many districts in the Bible Belt close for Good Friday. Legally this works because public-school calendars are set by local school boards operating under a state-granted minimum instructional-day floor; as long as the district hits its required days (typically 180), it can close on any day it chooses and call it a "holiday."

**Layer 4 — Employer holidays.** This is what most private-sector workers actually experience. The "typical" US full-time worker in a white-collar private-sector job gets roughly **8 paid holidays** per year, per BLS — essentially the federal calendar minus Columbus Day, MLK Day, and Veterans Day (the three that most private employers skip) plus sometimes a "floating" day or the day after Thanksgiving. Part-time, hourly, gig, and low-wage workers often get zero. Per BLS, 81% of civilian workers had access to paid holidays as of March 2022 ([BLS Economics Daily](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2023/higher-paid-workers-more-likely-than-lower-paid-workers-to-have-paid-leave-benefits-in-2022.htm)); the remaining ~19% — disproportionately the lowest wage quartile — have no paid holidays at all.

The three federal holidays that most private employers *skip* are MLK, Presidents Day, Columbus, and Veterans Day — which is exactly why the "average US worker gets 8 paid holidays" figure shakes out to roughly the federal 11 minus these.

**"Federal holiday" vs "national holiday":** There is no such thing as a US "national holiday" in any legal sense. The federal government cannot compel states, schools, or private employers to close on any day. The closest the US comes to a true national observance is Thanksgiving and Christmas — and those are cultural, not statutory.

## 3. PTO and paid leave in the US — the economic reality

This is the section that will actually matter for the school-calendar project, because it determines whether working parents have any slack to absorb a random out-of-school Tuesday.

### The headline numbers (BLS, March 2024/2025)

Sources: [BLS Employee Benefits in the United States, March 2024](https://www.bls.gov/ebs/publications/employee-benefits-in-the-united-states-march-2024.htm); [BLS Who Receives Paid Vacations](https://www.bls.gov/ebs/factsheets/paid-vacations.htm); [BLS EBS Table 6](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ebs2.t06.htm).

- **Paid holidays:** Available to ~79–81% of civilian workers. Average when provided: **~8 days/year**.
- **Paid vacation:** Available to ~77% of civilian workers. After 1 year of service: **~10–11 days** on average at small employers, **~14 days** at large employers. After 5 years: 14–18 days. After 20 years: 17–24 days.
- **Paid sick leave:** Available to ~79% of civilian workers (up sharply from the 2010s because of state-level mandates in ~17 states and DC).
- **Personal leave (separate from vacation/sick):** Available to only ~43% of civilian workers.

### Wage and industry breakdown — the inequality story

This is where the single-number averages are misleading. From BLS, higher-wage workers have dramatically more access ([BLS Economics Daily 2023](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2023/higher-paid-workers-more-likely-than-lower-paid-workers-to-have-paid-leave-benefits-in-2022.htm)):

- **Paid sick leave (2022):** 38% of workers in the **lowest 10% wage bracket** vs **96% in the highest 10%**.
- **Paid holidays** and **paid vacation** follow the same pattern — access roughly doubles between the bottom and top wage deciles.
- **Establishment size matters almost as much as wage:** 71% of workers in firms of 1–49 workers have paid vacation access, vs **91% in firms of 500+**.
- **Union workers** have markedly higher access across every category. (Part of the reason is that sectoral unions concentrate in industries that already have good benefits; part is the contract itself.)

### The OECD comparison

The US is the **only country in the OECD that does not legally mandate any paid annual leave**. Source: [CEPR, "No-Vacation Nation, Revised" (2019)](https://cepr.net/publications/no-vacation-nation-revised/). Key CEPR findings:

- **EU floor: 20 paid vacation days** (statutory minimum). Many countries go higher: UK 28 working days, Austria/Denmark/Finland/Sweden/Spain 25, Portugal 22.
- **US workers who do get PTO** average ~13 paid vacation days + 8 paid holidays. That is roughly what a European at the statutory minimum gets as vacation *alone*, not counting holidays.
- **Close to 1 in 4 US workers get neither paid vacation nor paid holidays**, and that share is concentrated in the bottom wage quartile, part-time workers, and small establishments.
- The US is also the only wealthy country without mandated **paid sick leave** at the national level ([CEPR Contagion Nation 2020](https://cepr.net/publications/contagion-nation-2020-united-states-still-the-only-wealthy-nation-without-paid-sick-leave/)).

Skeptic note: the "average 13 vacation + 8 holiday" figure has been cited for a decade. BLS 2024 data suggests the vacation number is nudging up at large employers (toward 14 at year 1) but has not moved meaningfully at small employers or among low-wage workers. The compositional story — who has what — has barely changed.

### PTO and school closures — direct research

- **Price & Wasserman (2023)** find that summer breaks reduce total work hours for women in the US — summarized in the [NBER w29641 working paper literature](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29641/w29641.pdf).
- **COVID-era evidence** is the cleanest natural experiment: when schools closed, **maternal labor force participation fell 3.4 percentage points**; when childcare facilities also closed, **6.1 points**. Mothers in non-essential, non-teleworkable jobs saw employment drops of **8–19 percentage points** ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9702976/), [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/202204/pandemic-school-closures-and-parents-labor-supply)).
- Mothers without college degrees absorbed the largest hour reductions.
- Fathers' employment barely moved in most of these studies.

The "care economy" literature (England, Folbre, and others) generalizes this: when school and childcare schedules create a gap, the shock is almost always absorbed by maternal labor supply — not paternal, not by purchased care — because the households most squeezed by the gap are also the ones that can least afford paid substitutes.

## 4. The mismatch — does US PTO cover US school calendars?

Back-of-the-envelope arithmetic. This is the number Gene's project is really about.

**A typical US school year** is 180 instructional days ([Pew 2023](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/09/07/in-the-u-s-180-days-of-school-is-most-common-but-length-of-school-day-varies-by-state/); [NCES state table](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/statereform/tab5_14.asp)). Out of ~260 weekdays per year, that leaves roughly **80 non-school weekdays** that a working parent with K–12 kids has to cover somehow. Break those ~80 down:

- **Summer:** ~50 weekdays (10 weeks × 5).
- **Winter break:** ~7–10 weekdays.
- **Spring break:** ~5 weekdays.
- **Thanksgiving week:** 2–3 extra weekdays beyond the federal holiday.
- **Federal/state/local holidays that fall on weekdays:** ~8–10.
- **Teacher workdays, in-service days, parent-teacher conferences, grading days, professional development:** typically **5–10 additional weekdays**, scattered unpredictably through the year. These are the "random Tuesday" days that are brutal for working parents, because they're fragmented and hard to plan around.

**The gap:**
- A US parent with roughly median private-sector benefits gets ~8 paid holidays + ~10–14 vacation days = **18–22 paid days off per year**. Many of these will be used for illness, travel, or family events unrelated to school closures.
- Against **~80 non-school weekdays**, that's a gap of roughly **55–60 uncovered weekdays per year, per child, even for a parent with above-median benefits**.
- For a parent in the **lowest wage quartile**, where ~25% have zero paid holidays and zero paid vacation, the gap is **~80 days. The entire non-school calendar is uncovered.**

**Who absorbs the gap?** The research is consistent:
1. **Partnered mothers** disproportionately reduce hours, take unpaid leave, shift to part-time, or exit the labor force. This is the dominant margin.
2. **Grandparents and informal kin networks** — especially in communities where extended family lives nearby.
3. **Unpaid leave under FMLA**, where eligible — but FMLA only covers employers with 50+ workers and employees who've worked 1,250+ hours, excluding most low-wage workers.
4. **Paid substitute care** (camps, day programs) — but a full summer of camp runs $3,000–$10,000+ per child in most metros, which is a non-starter below the ~60th income percentile.
5. **Single parents** with no absorber have the worst outcomes: the COVID evidence suggests job loss or forced downshifts to hourly/part-time work.

**The distributional bottom line:** the US school calendar is designed around a household model — typically a stay-at-home or part-time second earner, with one full-time breadwinner who has generous white-collar PTO — **that describes maybe 20–25% of households with K-12 kids today**. For the other 75–80%, every non-school weekday is an unfunded mandate.

## 5. Summer vacation — how it frames the whole picture

**The agrarian-roots story is wrong.** School in 19th-century rural America typically ran on a *two-term* calendar — a short winter term and a short summer term — because children were most needed at home in spring planting and fall harvest, not summer. Meanwhile, **urban 19th-century schools ran year-round**: NYC schools were open 248 days in 1842. Sources: [PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/education/debunking-myth-summer-vacation); [Kenneth Gold's history (EdWeek)](https://www.edweek.org/education/opinion-can-we-please-put-the-agrarian-roots-of-summer-vacation-myth-to-bed/2010/07).

**The real origin is urban.** In the mid-to-late 19th century, elite and then middle-class urban families began fleeing summer heat — which in pre-air-conditioning cities was genuinely dangerous, with disease outbreaks tracking the hot months. School attendance in summer collapsed among families who could afford to leave. Reformers pushed for calendar standardization, and the compromise was roughly the current model: drop the urban summer term, extend the rural school year so rural kids got more total instruction, and settle on a 180ish-day standard with a long summer. By 1889 most cities were running July-August summer breaks. Rural calendars caught up over the next few decades. So: **the ~10–12 week US summer break is an industrial-era urban-heat compromise, standardized between ~1840 and ~1900**, not a legacy of farm labor.

**The OECD comparison.** US summer breaks run ~10–12 weeks. Most OECD peers are shorter:
- **Germany:** ~6 weeks
- **UK:** ~6 weeks
- **Japan:** ~6 weeks
- **France:** ~7–8 weeks (closer to European average)
- **Nordic countries:** 6–10 weeks depending on country
- **Southern Europe** (Italy, Greece, Portugal): **10–13+ weeks**, often cited as climate-driven, same logic as the US

Sources: [OECD "How is the school year organised" (2024)](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2024/08/how-is-the-school-year-organised-in-oecd-countries_5ee6aec3/a6385722-en.pdf); [Euronews fact-check](https://www.euronews.com/my-europe/2025/02/10/fact-check-where-in-europe-has-the-longest-school-summer-holidays). Total school-year vacation across OECD averages ~14 weeks, ranging from under 11 in Denmark and Costa Rica to 17 in Greece, Latvia, and Lithuania.

**Potential cross-pattern worth checking in the existing dataset:** northern European countries combine **shorter summer breaks** with **more distributed shorter breaks** during the school year (half-term weeks, autumn breaks, longer Easter breaks). That's less summer "gap" but potentially *more* in-year fragmentation. The US mostly trades in the opposite direction: long summer, fewer distributed breaks. A district with an unusually long summer may or may not be "less fragmented" in-year — the correlation is worth testing with the dataset because it would answer whether districts are substituting summer length for in-year structure, or stacking both.

## 6. Key takeaways for the School Calendar Fragmentation project

1. **"Federal holiday" is a red herring for understanding school calendars.** Federal law closes banks, not schools. School closures are almost entirely a **state-floor-plus-local-discretion** phenomenon. The fragmentation in Gene's dataset is happening at layer 3 (district), not layer 1 (federal), and the district-level variation reflects cultural, religious, and administrative choices — not a top-down calendar.

2. **The US school calendar is designed for a household that barely exists anymore.** The 180-day, long-summer, sprinkled-workday calendar implicitly assumes one caregiver with flexible time. Maybe 20–25% of K-12 households fit that model. For everyone else — and especially the bottom wage quartile, where ~25% get zero paid holidays — every non-school weekday is an unfunded care mandate on the household. The fragmentation in Gene's dataset isn't just inconvenient; for tens of millions of working parents, it is a direct tax on labor supply, borne disproportionately by mothers and low-wage workers.

3. **The worst days aren't the long breaks — they're the scattered ones.** Summer and winter break are predictable and can be planned (camp, family, etc.). Teacher workdays, in-service days, and conference half-days are the real friction: they're short, frequent, unpredictable across districts, and exactly the kind of closures a salaried parent can absorb but an hourly parent cannot. A "fragmentation score" that weights scattered single-day closures higher than block breaks would be defensible on the research, not just the intuition.

4. **Inequality of PTO access → inequality of who can live with a fragmented calendar.** A district that adds Diwali, Eid, Lunar New Year, Good Friday, and two professional-development Fridays may be doing something culturally admirable and pedagogically sound, while simultaneously imposing 6+ additional days of unpaid care costs on the minority of its parents who lack PTO. These two effects aren't in conflict — they're both real — but the dataset can make that tradeoff visible in a way that the current school-calendar discourse doesn't.

5. **The "holiday system" is not a system.** It is four loosely coupled layers that were never designed to interlock with each other or with the school calendar. Most Americans — including most reporters covering this — assume it's coordinated. It isn't. The productive framing for Gene's project is: *show the seams*. Map where federal, state, local, and employer calendars diverge from the school calendar, and show who falls into the gap.
