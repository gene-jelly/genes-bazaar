# The Swiss Cheese School Year

## A Research Dossier on US School Calendar Fragmentation

> *This is a reference document. It is meant to be linked to, argued with, and corrected. The accompanying narrative essay cites back to this dossier; every number below has a source, and every source is footnoted. If you find an error, tell us.*

---

## Executive Summary

Across a diverse sample of 10+ US school districts, **roughly half of all school weeks are "broken"** — interrupted by a holiday, half-day, teacher in-service day, or early dismissal. The median district delivers **~49% unbroken five-day weeks**. The range runs from 40% (Philadelphia) to 61% (a reference private school).

Meanwhile, the average American full-time worker gets **~8 paid holidays per year**. The average K-12 student gets **~80 weekdays off per year**. Children have roughly **10x the holidays of their working parents** — a structural asymmetry that no one designed on purpose and no stakeholder group actively defends on the merits.

The causal chain is unglamorous: federal devolution of professional development authority (ESSA 2015) met the standard US teaching-load norm (~25+ classroom hours/week), and the only way to create collaborative time for teachers without reopening the teaching-load clause of their contracts was to subtract time from students. Every high-performing peer country — Finland, Singapore, Japan, Shanghai, Netherlands — embeds PD into the teacher's contracted week instead. None use the American model of recurring mid-week early release.

The economic cost is substantial but unevenly studied. A defensible lower bound — $55B/year in family childcare and lost wages — treats all days off the same. The **marginal cost of fragmentation specifically**, as distinct from block time-off, is not estimated in any published study. This is itself a finding: current calendar decisions are being made without an economic accounting of the fragmentation externality.

Fixes exist at every level of the system. The highest-leverage, lowest-friction move is a single school board vote to cluster professional development days adjacent to existing breaks (Fairfax County, VA will vote on exactly this on April 23, 2026). That alone can recover 6–10 broken weeks per year without touching a single contract clause.

**The hero numbers:**

| | |
|---|---|
| Median % of US school weeks that are unbroken | **~49%** |
| Ratio of student holidays to parent holidays | **~10:1** |
| Paid holidays, avg US full-time worker | **8/year** |

| Teaching hours/week, Finland (elementary) | **~18** |

| Districts with national "unbroken weeks" data | **0** |

## Part I: The Scope

### How many weeks of the school year are actually unbroken?

A "full week" is defined here as five consecutive Monday-through-Friday instructional days with no holiday, no early dismissal, no half-day, no teacher in-service day, and no modified schedule. The partial weeks at the start and end of the school year are excluded.

Counts below are derived from the official 2025–2026 calendars published by each district. Methodology is conservative: any half-day anywhere in the week disqualifies it.

| Reference: Urban Public B | Large urban public | Major NE city | 41 | **20** | **49%** |
| Fairfax County PS[^fairfax] | Suburban public | VA | ~41 | ~21 | **52%** |
| NYC DOE[^nyc] | Urban public | NY | 42 | ~19 | **45%** |

The pattern is near-universal. Fragmentation is not a private-school problem, a wealthy-district problem, or an urban problem. Every category lands in the 40–60% band. A week-by-week comparison of one private school and one urban public district — showing 25 vs. 20 unbroken weeks over the same calendar year — is available in [The Swiss Cheese School Year](./School%20Calendar%20Fragmentation.md).

Their first-grader gets 80+ weekdays off, and roughly half of their "school weeks" are punctured by a stray PD day or early dismissal that requires a childcare scramble. This is not an incidental mismatch. It is a **structural misalignment between two institutions — work and school — that are supposed to interlock**.

## Part II: How We Got Here

### ESSA 2015 and the devolution of PD authority

The Every Student Succeeds Act of 2015 replaced the No Child Left Behind accountability regime with a devolved model that gave districts broad discretion over how to spend federal Title II funds on teacher professional development. The policy intent was flexibility. The practical consequence, when combined with existing US teaching-load norms and collective-bargaining structures, was fragmentation.

Under the prior regime, PD was treated more as a federally-coordinated program; under ESSA it became a district-level scheduling problem. Districts needed to create teacher collaboration time inside contracts that did not contain such time. The path of least resistance was to subtract it from student contact hours — scheduling early-release Fridays, in-service Mondays, and conference half-days. These choices were each locally rational. The aggregate outcome is the swiss cheese calendar.

### The US teaching-load norm

American elementary teachers spend roughly 25–27 hours per week in direct classroom instruction — among the highest ratios in the OECD. Peer countries (discussed in Part V) run 15–20 hours/week. This 7–10 hour gap is, mechanically, where collaborative time can live. In Finland, Japan, Shanghai, and Singapore, it lives there. In the US, the contract doesn't contain it, so scheduling creates it by cutting student time.

This is why calendar fragmentation cannot be understood as a pedagogical choice. It is a **contractual artifact of the US teaching-load norm**, made visible when ESSA handed PD scheduling to districts. The industry press describes early-release days with unusual candor: "a compromise between administrators and teachers' unions to create more professional time within the working hours agreed to under the CBA."[^earlyrelease]

Nobody chose this. No research paper recommends it. No stakeholder group actively defends the scattered model on the merits. It is, in the phrase that captures it best: **a Nash equilibrium, not a design.**

## Part III: Who Pays

### The family cost

Estimates of the annual family cost of school days off vary by methodology, but the consensus lower bound — developed by Ready Nation, the Committee for Economic Development, and cited in Center for American Progress work — is approximately **$55 billion per year** in lost wages and childcare costs. This figure treats all days off uniformly and does not distinguish scattered from clustered time off.

The distribution of that cost is sharply gendered. Research on the four-day school week (Ward, SSRN 3301406, and follow-ups) finds that going from 0% to 25% four-day-week enrollment in an area causes an **~11% decline in employment among mothers of 5–13-year-olds**.[^ward] Maternal labor supply bears the brunt of calendar irregularity; paternal labor supply is roughly unaffected. One Minnesota district estimated ~$600/year in added childcare per family under a four-day-week regime.

The hourly-salaried gap is also severe, though less precisely measured. Schneider & Harknett's *Hard Times* (*Social Forces* 2021)[^harknett] finds that routine schedule unpredictability — whether from employers or from schools — produces material hardship (hunger, housing insecurity) independent of wages. Hourly workers cannot absorb fragmentation via flex time. They cannot pre-commit. Each scattered early-release day forces a live scramble, with fixed transaction costs per event (calls, transport, forfeited shifts). The Carrillo et al. PMC paper[^carrillo] documents parents "piecing together care at the last minute."

### The cognitive and developmental cost

Clinical research on children with ADHD and executive function differences is unusually robust on one point: **routine is a therapeutic intervention**. Roughly 90% of children with ADHD have executive function deficits; consistent routines are repeatedly identified as foundational to behavioral regulation, and unexpected changes disrupt "sense of structure and stability."[^adhd] Fragmentation hits these children harder than their peers — not marginally, but categorically — because the affordance (predictability) that the rest of the system takes for granted is the one thing their neurobiology requires to function.

There is no economic literature quantifying this disproportionate burden. This is whitespace.

### The cost of fragmentation, specifically

The question that matters for policy — **is scattered time off more costly than clustered time off, holding total days constant?** — has no direct published answer. Formal economics literature on calendar fragmentation as a distinct object does not exist. However, three adjacent lines of evidence triangulate strongly that the fragmentation premium is nonzero:

1. **Schroeter, Lalive & Karunanethy (IZA DP 17371, 2024)**[^schroeter] — the single closest analog. Using Swiss administrative data, they separate *anticipated* from *unanticipated* school closures and find that parents respond *strategically* to anticipated closures (planned leave, hours adjustment) but absorb unanticipated ones through "informal patches and stress." Same number of closure days, different economic incidence, entirely because of predictability. This does not directly compare block vs. scatter, but it establishes the mechanism.

2. **Mas & Pallais, "Valuing Alternative Work Arrangements" (AER 2017)**[^maspallais] — workers will give up an average of **20% of wages** to avoid an employer setting their schedule on short notice. Mothers of young children show among the highest willingness to pay. This is not a school-calendar number, but it is the closest available estimate of the *predictability premium* families place on schedule certainty.

3. **The Schneider-Harknett Shift Project infrastructure**[^harknett] — documents that schedule unpredictability produces material hardship independent of wages, and that it is regressive. The infrastructure exists to estimate school-calendar unpredictability costs directly. Nobody has pointed it there yet.

**The policy implication of the research gap is more interesting than any single number**: current district calendar decisions are made *without* an economic accounting of the fragmentation externality. The scattered model is treated as equivalent in cost to the same total days clustered. It almost certainly isn't. A single commissioned WTP study — a Shift-Project-style survey asking parents to value hypothetical calendars — would produce the first direct estimate and cost far less than the policy stakes.

## Part IV: The Professional Development Justification Doesn't Hold

The usual defense of scattered early-release days and mid-week in-service days is that they enable teacher professional development. The PD research itself does not support this defense.

### What the research actually says

The Learning Policy Institute's foundational review (Darling-Hammond et al., 2017)[^lpi] identifies seven features of effective PD, and consistently finds that **sustained duration** is non-negotiable. The floor for impact is roughly **50 hours focused on a single topic across 6–12 months**, delivered in forms that allow teachers to apply and refine practice between sessions. Scattered two-hour Friday-afternoon PD slots — the modal form in US districts — do not meet any of these criteria.

TNTP's *Mirage* report (2015)[^mirage] is the most-cited empirical challenge. Studying three large urban districts and one charter network, TNTP found that districts spend roughly **$18,000 per teacher per year** on PD while only **3 in 10 teachers** showed measurable improvement. No correlation between hours of PD and teacher improvement when the PD was fragmented. The report's phrase for the problem: "a mirage of improvement."

Put these together: the US PD regime spends enormous sums on a scattered-delivery model that the research explicitly identifies as the *least effective* delivery form, while imposing the entire calendar cost of that model on students and families. This is not a trade-off with merit on both sides. **The scattered model fails the cost-benefit test at both ends.**

### Why does it persist?

Because it is not pedagogy. It is a bargaining artifact. See Part II.

## Part V: What Peer Countries Do

Across 11 peer K-12 systems, nearly all embed teacher PD *into* teacher contract time — through reduced teaching loads, paid non-contact hours, and in-school collaborative structures. The US pattern of recurring mid-week early release has **no direct analog** in high-performing systems. Where student-free PD days exist abroad, they are typically 3–7 whole days bookending terms, not mid-week interruptions.

### The teaching-load gap

| Country | Teaching hours/week (elem) | PD mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Finland** | ~18 | 2 hours/week embedded in contract; 7 days avg PD taken annually |
| **Shanghai / China** | ~10–12 periods | Weekly Teaching Research Groups (*jiaoyanzu*), since 1952 |
| **Singapore** | ~20 | **100 hrs/year PD entitlement**, fully funded, plus 1 hr/week embedded peer learning |
| **Japan** | ~20 | Lesson Study embedded in after-school time; 99% of elementary participate |
| **Netherlands** | ~25 | By law: 459 hrs/year (of 1659) carved out for planning/meetings/PD |
| **Estonia** | ~19 | 20 days/year paid study leave, contractually mandated |
| **Germany** | ~25 | Multi-day block training at state academies, outside teaching time |
| **Ontario, Canada** | ~24 | 3–7 whole Professional Activity days (Reg. 304) at term edges |
| **Australia (Victoria)** | ~22 | Up to 5 pupil-free days plus Professional Practice Days (teacher out of class, students remain) |
| **England (UK)** | ~22 | 5 whole INSET days at term boundaries (the "Baker days") |
| **France** | ~24 | 2 half-days/year only; most PD on teacher own time |
| **United States** | **~25–27** | Scattered early-release, mid-week in-service, conference half-days |

The US sits at or above the top of the teaching-hours range and alone relies on mid-week student release to create PD time. The **7–10 hour weekly gap** between the US and peer systems like Finland is, mechanically, where those countries' PD lives. No gap exists in the US contract. So the time is taken from students.

### Specific embedded structures

- **Japan — Lesson Study (*jugyō kenkyū*)**. Teachers collaboratively plan a "research lesson," one teaches it while others observe, then they debrief and refine. 99% of elementary, 98% of junior high, 95% of high schools participate. Organized within salaried after-school time. District-wide research lessons use purposeful half-days, but these are semi-annual events, not weekly dismissals.[^lessonstudy]

- **Singapore — 100 hours/year PD entitlement**. Every teacher, every year, funded by the Ministry of Education. One hour per week of peer learning time is built into the school day with subject/grade groups. PD is **additive to teaching, not subtractive from student time**.[^singapore]

- **Shanghai — Teaching Research Groups (*jiaoyanzu*)**. Every school since 1952. Teachers grouped by subject, meeting weekly during contract time for collaborative lesson planning, open-lesson observation, and novice mentoring. Teaching load of 10–12 periods/week (vs. US ~25) is the scheduling affordance. This, not early release, is the structural feature behind Shanghai's TALIS and PISA advantage.[^shanghai]

- **Finland — 2 hours/week contractual PD time**. Elementary teachers contractually entitled to 1–5 paid PD days/year; average actually taken is ~7. Continuous school blocks with short autumn/Christmas/winter breaks and a ~10-week summer. No mid-week student dismissals.[^finland]

- **Victoria, Australia — Professional Practice Days**. The most interesting adaptation from a US-pragmatic standpoint. Teachers get a day *out of class* for planning, while **students remain in school staffed by relief teachers**. This releases teacher time without releasing student time. It exists inside a Western union context. It is the model most directly portable to US districts that can afford relief teacher coverage.[^victoria]

### The reform precedent: France 2013

In 2013, Education Minister Vincent Peillon implemented a reform **adding a half-day (Wednesday morning) back to the French primary school week**, reversing a prior 4-day structure. The explicit pedagogical rationale: the existing arrangement concentrated too many hours into too few days, produced excessive daily load, and **fragmented children's weekly routine**. The reform was controversial and partially rolled back in 2017, primarily for transport and extracurricular cost reasons, not pedagogy. As of late 2025, roughly one-third of (mostly rural) French primary schools operate on the 4-day week; the national debate is ongoing.[^france]

**This is the only clean reform precedent.** A peer democracy, inside a Western union context, explicitly de-fragmenting its student week for pedagogical reasons. No peer country has reformed *toward* US-style fragmentation. The direction of reform is unidirectional.

## Part VI: The Policy Landscape

### The Fairfax County case study

The most active US reform effort is in Fairfax County, Virginia. School board member **Mateo Dunne** has introduced a calendar reform proposal with a board vote **scheduled for April 23, 2026 — 19 days from this writing**. Dunne's public data includes: 52% full weeks in the current Fairfax calendar, a decade-long increase in non-instructional days from ~28 to ~40, and a **300% increase in early-release days since 2023**. His proposal centers on clustering PD days against existing breaks — a pure board-authority move requiring no contract reopener.[^dunne]

This vote is worth watching closely. It is effectively a live test of whether calendar consolidation can pass at the district level, in a large suburban system, via the political coalition of working parents (particularly mothers) against the status quo. Win or lose, it establishes a template.

### The Missouri funding lever

In 2024, Missouri passed HB 1569, offering a **1% state foundation aid bonus** to districts returning from four-day to five-day school weeks. This is a first-of-its-kind funding lever explicitly using state dollars to shape the calendar. The mechanism generalizes: a similar bonus could be structured around an "unbroken weeks" threshold rather than day count alone. No state has yet done this. Missouri's experiment is worth tracking for effect size before replication.[^missouri]

### The whitespace: no national coalition exists

There is **no dedicated national nonprofit** organizing around calendar fragmentation. *Start School Later*, the later-start-times advocacy organization founded by Terra Ziporyn Snider, is the closest template — single-issue, state-level legislative focus, pediatrician endorsement (AAP), working-mothers constituency. SSL has been the decisive lobbying force on school start times in ~10 states. A calendar-consolidation coalition could mirror this playbook directly.

The business community is silent. No Chamber of Commerce, no employer association, and no working-parents advocacy group has taken up calendar fragmentation as a workforce-participation issue, despite the ~$55B family cost and the disproportionate impact on maternal labor supply. This is striking. It means the political coalition for reform has not yet been assembled — not because it would be hard, but because nobody has done the basic organizing work.

### The missing dataset

No one maintains a national database of "unbroken weeks per district." For 13,000+ US school districts, there is no comparable data on calendar fragmentation. The Department of Education does not collect it. State departments of education do not publish it. Research organizations do not maintain it. The first deliverable of any reform coalition should be scraping every district's 2025–2026 calendar into a public database with standardized fragmentation metrics. **This is a weekend of work by a motivated developer**, and it does not exist.

## Part VII: The Fixes

A catalog of concrete interventions at seven levels of the system. Each entry: what it is, who acts, precedent, difficulty, impact, downsides.

### 1. Calendar Design Fixes (District/Board level)

**1a. Cluster PD days against existing breaks.** Move all teacher work days adjacent to Thanksgiving, winter, spring, or MLK breaks. No contract reopener needed if total work days unchanged. Precedent: Arlington VA, Montgomery County MD partial, Fairfax/Dunne current. **Difficulty: LOW-MEDIUM. Impact: VERY HIGH** — recovers 6–10 broken weeks per year. This is the single highest-leverage move available to any school board.

**1b. Eliminate weekly early-release days.** Kill the Wednesday 1pm (or equivalent) PD slot. Requires contract reopener. Precedent: several MA districts post-COVID, Denver debate 2023. **Difficulty: HIGH. Impact: VERY HIGH** — recovers ~36 fragmented weeks where it exists.

**1c. Move in-service to summer bookends.** Shift PD/work days to the week before and after school. Standard in most private schools and many Southern districts. **Difficulty: MEDIUM. Impact: HIGH.**

**1d. Align to county/regional master calendar.** Neighboring districts adopt a shared calendar so two-worker families aren't juggling mismatched early-release days. Maryland has de facto alignment; Hawaii (single-state district) is the extreme case. **Difficulty: MEDIUM. Impact: HIGH on family sanity, medium on unbroken weeks.**

### 2. PD Restructuring (replacing fragmentation with embedded time)

**2a. Common planning periods (Japan Lesson Study model).** Schedule all teachers in a grade/subject to share one free period daily. Requires schedule creativity, not more time. Precedent: Boston Teacher Residency schools, some Title I turnaround schools. **Difficulty: MEDIUM (schedule Tetris). Impact: HIGH.**

**2b. Longer school day with banked-time PD.** Add 15–20 minutes to each regular day; bank the minutes for weekly teacher blocks while students do enrichment. Precedent: Massachusetts ELT Initiative, Chicago 2012. **Difficulty: HIGH (day-length is core bargaining). Impact: HIGH.**

**2c. Specials-block PD (elementary).** Stack art/music/PE/library into a 90-min block so classroom teachers get weekly collaborative time without dismissing students. Common in charter networks (Uncommon, Success Academy). **Difficulty: LOW at building level; specialist FTE is the constraint. Impact: HIGH.**

**2d. Summer institute PD.** 3–5 days of concentrated summer PD with stipend, **in lieu of** scattered in-service days. Most large districts already run summer institutes; the question is whether they substitute or add. **Difficulty: LOW-MEDIUM. Impact: HIGH if substitutive.**

**2e. Professional Practice Days (Victoria, Australia model).** Release teacher time, not student time, using relief teachers. **This is the most interesting model for US pragmatic adoption.** Students stay in school; teachers rotate out for planning. **Difficulty: MEDIUM. Impact: HIGH, politically elegant.**

### 3. Labor / Contract Fixes

**3a. Contract language audit.** Identify the specific CBA clauses that lock in early-release (usually "weekly professional collaboration time" or "student contact day shall not exceed X minutes on day Y"). Fairfax FEA contract is publicly posted. **Difficulty: LOW to read, HIGH to renegotiate.**

**3b. Revenue-neutral calendar bargaining.** Offer teachers equivalent PD time in a different configuration (summer stipends, longer day, specials block) rather than asking them to give it up. Denver ProComp (2005) is the template for revenue-neutral restructuring. **Difficulty: MEDIUM. Impact: unblocks everything in Sections 1 and 2.**

**3c. Model contract language.** Have a reformist organization (NCTQ template, or a new coalition) publish turnkey calendar-consolidation contract language boards can adopt. **Difficulty: LOW. Impact: MEDIUM** (lowers transaction cost).

### 4. State Policy Levers (highest leverage tier)

**4a. Instructional-hours floor with anti-fragmentation teeth.** State law requiring X instructional hours/week, with language preventing hours from being met via padded short weeks. The 32-hours/week threshold is research-supported (below which learning degrades) but not codified anywhere. Precedent: Texas (75,600 min/year), California SB 98, Missouri 2024. **Difficulty: MEDIUM. Impact: VERY HIGH** — forces calendar redesign statewide.

**4b. Calendar approval requirement.** Require districts to submit calendars to the state board for approval against fragmentation criteria (e.g., max 2 single-day interruptions per semester). **Difficulty: MEDIUM. Impact: HIGH.**

**4c. Funding-formula bonus (Missouri model, generalized).** 1–3% foundation aid bonus for districts meeting a "full weeks" standard. Easier politically than sticks. **Difficulty: LOW-MEDIUM. Impact: MEDIUM.**

**4d. Mandatory calendar transparency dashboard.** State-run public dashboard showing every district's unbroken weeks, early-release count, in-service days. Name-and-shame as policy, and also creates the comparable data that currently does not exist. **Difficulty: LOW. Impact: MEDIUM, foundational.**

### 5. Federal Policy (weakest tier)

**5a. ESSA Title I reporting amendment.** Include calendar fragmentation metrics on state report cards. **Difficulty: HIGH (ESSA reauth is slow). Impact: LOW-MEDIUM.**

**5b. IES research priority.** Fund studies on calendar fragmentation's achievement and family-cost effects. Creates the evidence base reformers currently lack. **Difficulty: LOW. Impact: slow but foundational.**

The honest assessment: **federal levers are genuinely weak on this issue.** The action is at the state and local level. Do not oversell federal reform.

### 6. Parent / Civic Organizing

**6a. Form a "Full Weeks Coalition" on the Start School Later template.** Single-issue, state chapters, model legislation on retainer, pediatrician endorsement, working-mothers as core constituency. SSL was founded by one person (Terra Ziporyn Snider). **Difficulty: MEDIUM to start, HIGH to sustain. Impact: structural.**

**6b. Scrape the missing dataset.** Build a public database of every district's unbroken-weeks count. Weekend of work for a motivated developer. First deliverable of any serious coalition.

**6c. School board candidate recruitment.** Recruit and fund board candidates running on calendar reform as a single issue. Moms for Liberty demonstrated the template (regardless of politics). **Difficulty: LOW to recruit. Impact: VERY HIGH per win** — boards control the calendar.

**6d. Employer-partnership petition.** Chamber of Commerce endorsement framing fragmented calendars as workforce-participation drag. Chambers supported universal pre-K on the same logic. **Difficulty: LOW. Impact: changes the coalition from parents-vs-teachers to employers+parents-vs-status-quo.**

### 7. Employer-Side Fixes

**7a. Corporate calendar alignment.** Large employers designate school in-service days as flex/WFH days by default. Patagonia offers this; some tech firms have school-holiday = company-holiday policies. **Difficulty: LOW per firm. Impact: indirect on calendar itself, large on family burden.**

**7b. Employer-sponsored in-service camps.** Large employers or employer consortia fund drop-in camps on in-service days. **Warning: this is palliative, not a fix.** It may reduce parent pressure and thereby prop up the fragmented calendar.

**7c. Chamber of Commerce resolutions.** Local Chambers pass resolutions calling for consolidated calendars. Tulsa Chamber engaged on the four-day-week debate. **Difficulty: LOW. Impact: political cover for reform boards.**

### Actionable "this year" priorities

**For a parent:** (1) attend every school board calendar vote; (2) file a FOIA/open-records request for the district's bargaining agreement, find the calendar clause; (3) recruit 10 families to testify at the next calendar approval meeting; (4) connect with Fairfax/Dunne's team for playbook.

**For a board member:** (1) put Fix 1a (cluster PD days) on the next agenda — pure board authority, no reopener; (2) commission an audit of where PD actually happens and whether it's effective — if it isn't, the "we need early-release for PD" defense collapses; (3) pilot Fix 2c (specials-block PD) at one elementary school.

**For a state legislator:** (1) introduce Fix 4c (Missouri-style bonus — politically easy); (2) pair with Fix 4d (transparency dashboard — even easier); (3) hold a hearing with working-mother witnesses to build the coalition for Fix 4a next session.

## Research Gaps (Whitespace)

These are findings, not just limitations. The fact that each of these does not exist is itself policy-relevant.

1. **No national dataset of "unbroken weeks per district."** For 13,000+ districts. Nobody is collecting it. Weekend of work to build, does not exist.
2. **No direct estimate of the "fragmentation premium"** — the marginal cost of scattered vs. clustered time off, holding total days constant. The $55B base figure doesn't decompose.
3. **No ADHD-specific economic literature** on calendar disruption, despite robust clinical evidence that routine is a therapeutic intervention.
4. **No willingness-to-pay survey** for school calendar predictability. Mas-Pallais covered employer discretion; the school-calendar analog is obvious and unrun.
5. **No child-outcome study** comparing fragmented vs. clustered 180-day calendars at equal instructional minutes. Summer learning loss is well-studied; within-year fragmentation effects are not.
6. **No precise "unbroken weeks" metric for peer countries.** Eurydice publishes calendars but nobody has computed the comparable metric. Would strengthen the international argument.
7. **No peer-reviewed study isolating mid-year fragmentation** (as distinct from four-day weeks or total instructional time).

Each gap is a research project. Several are inexpensive. None are politically blocked. The whitespace is real and is recruitable.
