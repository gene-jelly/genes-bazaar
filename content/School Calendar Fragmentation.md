# The Swiss Cheese School Year

**How many weeks of the school year are *actually* unbroken, five-days-in-school weeks?**

This is a question people rarely quantify but deeply feel. Every parent knows the rhythm: just as the family settles into a groove — homework routines locked, morning chaos tamed — along comes a teacher in-service day, a random Monday holiday, or a half-day Friday that breaks the spell.

We decided to count. Week by week, across the entire 2025–2026 school year, for three reference points:

1. **A private K–12 school** in a major Northeastern city
2. **The local public school district** in the same city
3. **The national average** — based on published research and a 180-day standard

The results are worse than you'd guess.

---

## The Method

We went through every Monday-through-Friday block from the first day of school to the last. A week is **broken** if *any* day has no school, a half day, an early dismissal, or a modified schedule. We're asking: **how many weeks do kids go to school all five days, full days, no asterisks?**

Sources: Official 2025–26 calendars from both schools, Pew Research Center (2023), Fairfax County Public Schools analysis (2025).

---

## The Results

| Metric | Private School | Public District | National Avg |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unbroken 5-day weeks** | **25** | **20** | **22–25** |
| Total calendar weeks | 41 | 42 | ~40 |
| % of weeks that are full | **61%** | **48%** | ~52–63% |
| Longest unbroken streak | 5 weeks | 6 weeks | ~3–4 weeks |
| Total non-school days | ~36 | ~44 | ~30–40 |

**At the public district, more than half of all school weeks have at least one interruption.** At the private school, it's better — but still, nearly 4 in 10 weeks aren't full weeks.

## What's Killing the Public District's Number?

The difference between 25 and 20 full weeks isn't about total days off — it's about **distribution**.

**The public district has 8 early-dismissal Fridays** scattered throughout the year for teacher professional development. Each one wrecks an otherwise full week. The private school bundles its teacher days into full closures alongside existing breaks. That approach is more disruptive per event but **breaks fewer total weeks**.

The public district also observes more individual holidays: Veterans Day, Election Day (twice — November and May), and additional religious holidays. Plus a post-Thanksgiving Monday vacation day that turns a 2-day break into a week-breaker by contaminating the following week.

Meanwhile, the private school's calendar has its own quirks. A **two-week spring break** (March 23 – April 3) is generous by any standard — the public district gives one week. And the private school takes the full Thanksgiving week off where the public district only closes Thursday–Friday (but then takes Monday of the following week, breaking *two* weeks instead of one).

### The "Death by a Thousand Cuts" Pattern

The total non-school days are similar (~36 vs ~44). But the public district distributes them in the most fragmenting way possible — a half-day Friday here, an Election Day Tuesday there, a lone clerical day on a Thursday. Each one is small. Together, they turn 48% of the school year into interrupted weeks.

## How Does This Compare Nationally?

The best published data comes from a [Fairfax County (VA) analysis](https://www.fairfaxtimes.com/articles/fix-the-calendar-fairfax-families-need-consistent-school-weeks/article_38ad9791-4656-483e-a77e-9e0699b01977.html) — one of the only districts to publicly quantify this problem. They found that **only 52% of school weeks are full 5-day weeks**, and that the number of non-instructional days has risen from ~28 to ~40 per year over the past decade.

Our public district maps almost exactly to this figure (48%). The private school (61%) is better than average. The national picture, based on the standard 180-day school year:

- **180 instructional days** = 36 weeks of school (the [national standard](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2023/09/07/in-the-u-s-180-days-of-school-is-most-common-but-length-of-school-day-varies-by-state/) in 27 states)
- Those 36 weeks are spread across **~40–42 calendar weeks** (late August through mid-June)
- **~30–40 non-instructional days** are embedded in that span (holidays, breaks, PD days, conferences)
- Those days are scattered across **15–20 different weeks**, breaking each one
- Estimated **22–25 unbroken weeks** in a typical district

There is no comprehensive national study on this metric. The Fairfax data is the most specific published figure. This analysis may be one of the first to compare private vs. public calendars week-by-week.

## The Longest Streaks

Perhaps the most telling metric: how many consecutive full weeks can kids string together?

| Period | Private School | Public District |
|---|---|---|
| Longest streak | **5 weeks** (Apr 20–May 22) | **6 weeks** (Apr 6–May 15) |
| 2nd longest | 3 weeks (three times) | 2 weeks (several times) |

Ironically, the public district's longest streak (6 weeks) is longer than the private school's (5 weeks). Spring is where both calendars finally leave kids alone — the stretch between spring break and Memorial Day is the calmest part of the year.

But in the fall? From Labor Day through Thanksgiving, the private school manages three 2–3 week streaks. The public district never gets past 2 consecutive full weeks. The early-dismissal Fridays make it impossible.

---

## So What?

This isn't a critique of any particular school. Holidays matter. Teacher professional development matters. Religious observances matter. The question is whether the *distribution* of these days is optimal.

A school that clusters its non-instructional days around existing breaks preserves more unbroken weeks. A school that scatters them evenly (like monthly early-dismissal Fridays) maximizes the number of weeks that feel fragmented.

For families — especially those managing childcare, work schedules, and the routines that help kids (and parents) with ADHD — the difference between 20 and 25 full weeks is enormous. That's five more weeks per year where the rhythm holds.
