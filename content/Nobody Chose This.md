# Nobody Chose This

> *This is the companion essay to [The Swiss Cheese School Year: A Research Dossier](./School%20Calendar%20Fragmentation%20%E2%80%94%20Research%20Dossier.md). Every claim below is sourced in the dossier. This essay makes one argument; the dossier holds the receipts.*

---

The average American worker gets eight paid holidays a year.

Their first-grader gets eighty weekdays off.

That's a ten-to-one ratio between the number of holidays the people who don't work get and the number of holidays the people who do work get. No committee chose that ratio. No legislature voted on it. No school board deliberated over whether children should have ten times the holidays of their parents. It is simply the residue of a hundred small, locally-rational decisions colliding with each other across a hundred thousand school districts and arriving, somehow, here.

And it is not just the total. It is the *distribution*.

Across a diverse sample of American school districts — New York City, Los Angeles, Chicago, Houston, Philadelphia, Detroit, a wealthy New York suburb, a large independent private school, a high-performing charter network — **roughly half of all school weeks are broken**. The median district delivers about 49% unbroken, five-day weeks. Philadelphia comes in at 40%. The best-performing school in the sample, a well-resourced private school, hits 61% — meaning that even in the best case, nearly four in ten weeks are interrupted by a holiday, an in-service day, a parent-teacher conference half-day, or an early dismissal for teacher professional development.

Every working parent recognizes this rhythm. The morning routines finally lock in. The homework system starts working. The weekend grocery run is dialed. And then, on a Wednesday in October, there's a half-day. The following week, a random Monday is out for "professional development." Two weeks later, an early dismissal Friday. Three weeks after that, a full day off for a holiday you forgot was on the calendar. Each disruption is small. Each one, individually, is defensible. Together, they produce a school year in which *the rhythm never fully holds* — and the cost of that absence falls on exactly the people least equipped to absorb it: hourly workers, single parents, and the families of children whose neurology requires routine as a therapeutic intervention.

## The obvious question

Who decided this?

When I went looking for the answer, I expected villains. I expected to find a well-defended position, a stakeholder group with a coherent argument for why scattered mid-week early-release days and random in-service Mondays are actually good for children's education or for teachers' professional growth. I expected to have to rebut it.

I could not find one.

**No research paper recommends the scattered model.** The most authoritative review of teacher professional development, from the Learning Policy Institute, identifies sustained duration — roughly 50 hours focused on a single topic across 6–12 months — as non-negotiable. Scattered two-hour Friday-afternoon slots do not meet any of the seven criteria the research identifies as necessary. TNTP's *Mirage* report, studying three large districts and a charter network, found that scattered PD consumes about $18,000 per teacher per year while only three in ten teachers show measurable improvement. No correlation between hours spent and outcomes, when the hours are fragmented.

**No union publicly defends scattered delivery.** Early-release days are, to be blunt, embarrassing for unions to defend on pedagogical grounds, because they were never pedagogical in origin. The industry press describes them with unusual candor: "a compromise between administrators and teachers' unions to create more professional time within the working hours agreed to under the CBA."

**No parent group advocates for calendar fragmentation.** Parents of every political orientation, when surveyed, want more school-day predictability, not less. The childcare industry doesn't want it. The business community isn't agitating for it.

**And no peer country uses the American model.** Finland, Singapore, Japan, Shanghai, Germany, the Netherlands, Estonia, Ontario, Victoria, England, France — none of them rely on recurring mid-week student dismissal for teacher PD. Where whole student-free PD days exist abroad, they exist as 3–7 whole days bookending terms, not as scattered mid-week holes.

So the question reframes itself. Not "who chose this," but: **if nobody chose it, why is it here?**

## The hidden mechanism

Here is the thing I did not expect to find, and the thing that I think matters most.

In every high-performing peer country, teachers have something American teachers do not: **non-teaching hours built into their contracted week**.

A Finnish elementary teacher teaches roughly 18 hours a week of direct classroom instruction. A Shanghai Teaching Research Group member teaches 10–12 periods a week. A Dutch teacher's annual contract explicitly carves out 459 of 1659 hours for planning, meetings, and professional development. In Japan, 99% of elementary schools run "Lesson Study" collaborations inside the after-school window of the normal school day. In Singapore, every teacher is entitled to 100 fully-funded hours of professional development per year, plus one hour per week of peer learning built into the school day itself.

American elementary teachers teach 25 or more hours a week of direct instruction. The contract does not contain the non-teaching envelope. The teacher's workday has been, in effect, fully allocated to students.

When the Every Student Succeeds Act passed in 2015 and devolved teacher professional development scheduling to individual districts, each district had to answer a question that peer countries had already solved structurally: *where does the collaborative time for teachers come from?*

In Finland, the answer was: from the non-teaching hours we built into the contract.
In the United States, the answer was: from the students.

This is not a metaphor. It is, mechanically, what happened. The only way to create teacher collaborative time without re-opening the teaching-load clause of a collective bargaining agreement — the clause that governs how many classroom hours a teacher owes per week — was to reach inside the student contact day and cut time out. Early-release Fridays. Half-day Wednesdays. Scattered in-service Mondays. Conference half-days. Each one is not a pedagogical decision. Each one is a workaround around a contract that doesn't contain something the job actually requires.

Nobody chose the American school calendar. **The calendar is what you get when you subtract collaborative teacher time from a contract that doesn't include it.** The swiss-cheese pattern, the ten-to-one asymmetry, the interrupted rhythm that makes working parents tear their hair out — all of it is the downstream consequence of a single structural gap between the American teaching-load norm and the peer-country teaching-load norm.

This diagnosis matters because of what it implies about the fix.

If the problem were bad pedagogy, you'd need to change minds. If it were entrenched unions, you'd need political war. If it were incompetent administration, you'd need to replace people. It is none of these things. It is a hidden clause in a contract that nobody remembers writing, and the fix is to make the clause visible and rewrite it.

## The reform that exists

Here is the part of the story that gave me genuine hope when I found it, because I had been assuming that "no peer country has ever reformed toward or away from fragmentation" was the kind of inertial fact that produces despair.

It isn't true.

In 2013, French Education Minister Vincent Peillon implemented a reform *explicitly adding a half-day back to the French primary school week* — reversing a prior 4-day structure — for the specific pedagogical reason that the 4-day arrangement fragmented children's weekly rhythm and concentrated too many hours into too few days. The reform was controversial. It was partially rolled back in 2017, for transport and extracurricular-cost reasons, not for pedagogy. But it happened. A peer democracy, operating inside a Western union context, decided that the fragmentation of the student week was worth reversing, and reversed it.

That is the only clean precedent in the modern record. There is no case, anywhere, of a peer country reforming *toward* American-style fragmentation. The direction of reform is unidirectional. The Americans built the outlier; the French are the only ones who tried to un-build it.

## What one vote can do

Nineteen days from when I'm writing this, on April 23, 2026, a school board in Fairfax County, Virginia will vote on a calendar reform proposal put forward by board member Mateo Dunne.

The proposal doesn't touch the teacher contract. It doesn't require federal legislation. It doesn't need a state law. It doesn't ask anyone to give anything up. It simply clusters teacher professional development days *adjacent to existing breaks* — moving a scattered November Monday next to Thanksgiving, a February Tuesday next to Presidents' Day, a March Wednesday next to spring break — so that the same total number of teacher work days no longer interrupts the same number of student weeks.

Dunne's own district data: 52% full weeks in the current Fairfax calendar, a decade-long increase in non-instructional days from 28 to 40, a 300% increase in early-release days since 2023. Working parents notice. He is the first board member I know of to name the pattern publicly, quantify it precisely, and propose a fix that can pass with a single board vote.

If the Fairfax vote succeeds, it establishes a template. A single school board, using the authority it already has, can reclaim six to ten broken weeks per year without touching a single contract clause. Every school board in the country has this power. None are using it. Nineteen days from now, we'll find out whether one of them starts.

## What you can do tomorrow morning

The research [dossier](./School%20Calendar%20Fragmentation%20%E2%80%94%20Research%20Dossier.md) catalogs more than twenty concrete fixes at seven levels of the system — from calendar design to contract language to state funding formulas to international-style embedded PD structures. Some are hard. Some require years of coalition-building. Some require a statehouse.

But the one at the top is not any of those. The one at the top is:

**Put Fix 1a on your school board's next agenda.** *Cluster PD days adjacent to existing breaks.*

It requires no contract reopener. It does not ask teachers to work more hours. It does not cut instructional minutes. It does not cost money. It does not require state or federal action. It is a calendar decision inside the existing authority of every elected school board in America. And it can recover six to ten broken weeks per year in a single vote.

You don't have to run for office to get this done. You don't have to lobby a legislature. You don't have to build a coalition. You have to attend a single school board meeting, during public comment, and ask your board members — by name — to put calendar clustering on the next agenda. Bring this essay, or bring the dossier, or bring your own week-by-week count of your district's 2025–2026 calendar. Ask them whether they can name the pedagogical justification for the current distribution. Watch them try.

If enough parents, in enough districts, did only that one thing, it would be the largest single act of calendar reform in American public education history. And it would cost roughly forty-five minutes.

## The generous diagnosis

I want to close with the posture that the evidence actually supports, because the temptation in writing about any entrenched policy failure is to find someone to be angry at.

There is nobody to be angry at.

The teachers did not choose this. They are, in most cases, the people most frustrated by it, because their collaborative time is the thing that gets scattered. The administrators did not choose this; they inherited a contract structure that forced their hand. The unions did not choose it as a pedagogical preference; they chose it as the least-bad option inside a constraint they did not write. The school boards have largely not even *noticed* it, because the metric — "unbroken five-day weeks per year" — is not on any state report card and not in any federal dataset and not on any dashboard a board member ever sees.

The American school calendar is what happens when many reasonable people solve a small problem many times, locally, inside a constraint nobody can see and nobody remembers designing. It is a failure mode of locality, not of character.

Which means the fix is also local. And already legal. And already sitting unclaimed on the agenda of every school board meeting in America, waiting for one parent to ask.
