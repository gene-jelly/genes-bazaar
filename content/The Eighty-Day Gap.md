The arithmetic is crude, and the crudeness is the point.

A typical US school year has **180 instructional days**. The US calendar has about **260 weekdays**. Subtract one from the other and you get the number of weekdays per year when a working parent with K–12 kids has to do something — anything — to cover the gap between the economy, which mostly runs Monday through Friday, and the school system, which doesn't.

**That number is about 80.**

Eighty weekdays. Out of 260. Roughly **one working weekday in three** is a school-closed day.

Now the other side. The median US private-sector worker who *has* paid leave — which already excludes about a quarter of the workforce — gets approximately **8 paid holidays and 10 to 14 paid vacation days per year**. Call it **20**. Many of those will be used for things that have nothing to do with school closures: a parent's illness, a funeral, a rare family trip, a sick kid on a day school is in session.

Eighty minus twenty is sixty. **A median US worker's PTO covers roughly one out of every four non-school weekdays their child faces per year.** For the other three, something else has to happen — informal family care, an unpaid day off, a call to a neighbor, a reduction in hours, a quiet exit from the labor force.

Do this same math for a parent in the **lowest wage quartile**, where [about 25% of workers have zero paid holidays and zero paid vacation](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2023/higher-paid-workers-more-likely-than-lower-paid-workers-to-have-paid-leave-benefits-in-2022.htm). The gap is not 60 days. It is **80**. The entire non-school calendar is uncovered.

This is what the [school calendar fragmentation dataset](./The%20Districts%20Already%20Count%20This.md) is actually measuring, underneath the numbers. It is measuring the size of the unfunded care mandate that the US school calendar imposes on working parents, and it is measuring it one district at a time, across 5.6 million kids, to show that *no district in the country's top 23 has designed its calendar around the possibility that both parents in a household work*.

## How the 80 breaks down

The 80 non-school weekdays per year aren't a single block. They decompose into several types of closures, each with its own planning signature:

| Type | Typical weekdays | Can a working parent plan for it? |
|---|---|---|
| Summer break | ~50 | Yes, with months of notice |
| Winter break | ~7–10 | Yes |
| Spring break | ~5 | Yes |
| Thanksgiving week | 2–3 beyond the holiday itself | Yes |
| Federal/state holidays (weekday) | ~8–10 | Often aligned with employer holidays |
| Teacher workdays, in-service days, conferences, half-days | ~5–15 | **No — these are the brutal ones** |

The first five rows are the kind of closures that are *planned around* — summer camps, family travel, holiday childcare arrangements, grandparent rotations. They cost money, often a lot of money, but the cost is absorbable because it's predictable. A household with a stable plan can budget for a $4,000 summer of camp. A household without the budget knows well in advance they'll be scrambling.

The last row is the category that makes the fragmentation dataset interesting. The scattered teacher workdays, in-service days, parent-teacher conference half-days, and random professional development Fridays are the closures that **cannot be planned around** by any working parent who doesn't have fully flexible time. And they are exactly the closures that the Phase 1 dataset counts, because they're exactly the ones that disqualify a week from being "unbroken."

When [Philadelphia](./The%20Districts%20Already%20Count%20This.md) has 16 scattered early-release days in a single school year, that's not 16 minor inconveniences. That's 16 distinct household crises for every family in the district that has an hourly worker, no PTO, or a job that can't be "worked from home at 11:30." Multiply by 100,000+ such families in a large urban district and you have an annual disruption budget measured in seven figures of unpaid labor and lost wages — almost entirely absorbed by mothers.

## Who actually absorbs the gap

The research here is extensive and the direction is unambiguous. When school and childcare schedules collide with work schedules, the shock is almost entirely absorbed by mothers.

- **Price & Wasserman (2023)** find that summer breaks directly reduce total work hours for women in the US, summarized in [NBER w29641](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29641/w29641.pdf).
- **COVID-19 school closures** produced the cleanest natural experiment in decades: [when schools closed, maternal labor force participation fell 3.4 percentage points](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9702976/). When childcare facilities also closed, the drop was **6.1 points**. Mothers in non-essential, non-teleworkable jobs — the ones most likely to be hourly, low-wage, and without paid leave — saw employment drops of **8 to 19 percentage points**.
- **Mothers without college degrees** absorbed the largest hour reductions.
- **Fathers' employment barely moved in any of these studies.** This is not a story about parents. It is a story about mothers.
- **Ward (2019, [SSRN 3301406](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3301406))** estimates that when a quarter of students in an area attend 4-day-week schools, *maternal* employment drops by **11 percent**. A 25% penetration of a predictable four-day schedule produces a double-digit labor supply shock. The comparable effect of the scattered-day regime — which on the [Goodman 2014 mechanism](./The%20Half-Day%20Is%20Worse%20Than%20the%20Whole%20Day.md) is strictly more disruptive per hour than the 4-day week, because it is uncoordinated — has never been estimated, because nobody has had a cross-district dataset to estimate it from.

The research dossier behind this project has the fuller literature review. The shortest version is: **every rigorous study that has looked at school schedule disruptions and maternal labor supply has found large effects**, and those effects are concentrated in exactly the households that the school calendar's scattered-day regime hits hardest.

## The coverage map, one more time

Here is the inequality story in one picture:

| Worker category | Paid holidays | Paid vacation | Covered / 80 | Uncovered |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Top wage decile, large firm | ~10 | ~17–24 | ~27–34 | ~46–53 |
| Median private-sector full-time | ~8 | ~10–14 | ~18–22 | ~58–62 |
| Bottom wage decile, small firm | 0–4 | 0–5 | 0–9 | **~71–80** |
| US median *hourly* worker without union | Varies widely, often near zero | Often near zero | 0–15 | ~65–80 |

Read the right column. For a top-decile worker at a 500+ person firm, **roughly half the school calendar's non-school weekdays are covered** by paid leave. For the median worker, less than a third. For the bottom quartile, **almost none of it**.

Now read the left column. The top-decile worker does not, in fact, have more *hours* in the day than the bottom-decile worker. They have the same hours. What they have more of is **money**. They can buy camp. They can hire after-school. They can hire a nanny to cover the 3 p.m. early release. The school calendar's non-school weekdays don't actually go away for the top quartile; they are *priced away* — converted from a time problem into a money problem and then handed to the paid-care labor market, which is itself staffed by the people at the bottom of the distribution who cannot price their own away.

The school calendar, in effect, runs on **transferring 60 uncovered weekdays per year per child from the bottom of the labor market to the top via the care economy**. And when the care economy can't absorb it — because the household can't afford to buy in — the transfer goes the other direction: **the bottom of the labor market eats it**, in the form of reduced hours, unpaid leave, or exit.

This is the unfunded mandate. It is hiding in plain sight because nobody quantifies it — partly because the school calendar data doesn't exist in a comparable cross-district form ([until now](./The%20Districts%20Already%20Count%20This.md)), and partly because the PTO data and the school calendar data live in completely different disciplines that rarely talk.

## Why this is not a conservative critique and not a liberal critique

The instinct on the right will be to read this essay as a complaint about the public school system, which should accept more calendar discipline and stop imposing costs on families. That reading is plausible from paragraph one but collapses at paragraph twenty, because the biggest source of scattered closures is not the school system's incompetence — it is the **layering of religious, cultural, and professional-development observances** that many of these districts have added for good reasons. NYC adding Lunar New Year, Diwali, Eid al-Fitr, and Eid al-Adha is *culturally admirable and pedagogically sound*. It is also five additional unfunded care days per year for the 25% of NYC households with zero PTO. Both things are true. The right-leaning reader has to grapple with the fact that the calendar is doing something genuinely good and simultaneously producing a regressive tax on working parents.

The instinct on the left will be to read this as a complaint about American labor law, which should mandate paid leave the way every other OECD country does. That reading is also plausible and is strongly supported by the [CEPR "No-Vacation Nation"](https://cepr.net/publications/no-vacation-nation-revised/) series. But it collapses at a different point: **even European PTO floors would not fully close the gap.** The EU statutory minimum is 20 working vacation days. Add roughly 10 holidays and you get 30 — still short of 80. European working parents close the remaining gap because European school calendars also have shorter summers (6–7 weeks vs 10–12 in the US) and because European countries also have subsidized public childcare infrastructure that the US does not. The left-leaning reader has to grapple with the fact that mandated PTO is a *piece* of the fix, not a complete one. The school calendar has to change *alongside* the labor policy, or all that happens is working parents get a few more days to spend on a calendar that is still structurally hostile to them.

The honest reading is that the mismatch is *structural*, it was never designed, it compounds across four uncoordinated layers (federal holiday law, state holiday law, local school calendars, employer PTO policies), and nobody owns the problem because it isn't located anywhere. It's the seam between systems.

This project is, in one sense, just **showing the seam**.

## The number you came here for

If you want a single takeaway sentence to carry away from this essay:

**The median American worker's paid leave covers about a quarter of the school calendar's non-school weekdays. The bottom quartile gets zero coverage. The gap is absorbed, overwhelmingly, by mothers reducing their hours.**

The [fragmentation dataset](../data/school-calendars/districts.csv) makes this visible district by district. The mechanism story — [why scattered disruptions are structurally worse than whole closures](./The%20Half-Day%20Is%20Worse%20Than%20the%20Whole%20Day.md) — makes it visible at the classroom level. And the PTO inequality data makes it visible at the household level.

Three views of the same thing. The thing is the seam.
